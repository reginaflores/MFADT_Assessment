MFADT Curriculum Assessment Dashboard

This is a curriculum assessment project for the MFA Design & Technology (MFADT) program at Parsons School of Design, covering the 2025–2026 academic year. Now in its fourth year since the last major curriculum change, this assessment evaluates whether program-level learning outcomes are being achieved.

Interview Guide
Student Questions (Qualitative)

Qualitative interview guide for graduating students. Three sections, structured but semi-improvised — the interviewer (PTF) adapts based on student responses.

1 — Expectations & Aspirations

With what ambition did a student join the DT program, and are they able to successfully achieve that goal?

  • What was your background prior to DT (professional experience and/or undergraduate degree)?
  • Were you / have you been able to accomplish your goals for your time in DT?
  • How did your goals change based on your time here?

2 — Growth & Challenges

What fosters growth within the DT program — inside and outside the classroom?

  • How did you build upon your background during your time in DT?
  • Name one existing practice or activity/assignment that most contributed to your growth and/or development, creatively or collaboratively. How?
  • If you were to eliminate one activity or assignment from your DT required courses, what would that be? Why?

3 — Areas of Improvement

How do we continue to grow as a program and meet changing student requirements?

  • What are one or two ways we could better support your critical and conceptual skills?
  • If you could give the program any one thing in terms of specific support or resource, what would that be?

What students named as valuable

Major Studio (13) dominates. MS-1 in particular is praised for its iterative feedback structure, milestone-based pacing, and signature assignments — Instruction Sets for Strangers and the five-and-five quick-prototyping midterm are both named directly. Specific mentors (Emo, Harpreet, Diane Ruby) are credited as often as the course itself.

xFab (11) is valued less for any single unit than for the broader "scrappy making mindset" — physical computation, 3D printing, and the final integrative project surface most often. Several students name xFab's social culture and the faculty (Jane, Sam, Nancy) as the actual value.

Critical Computation (8) is praised mainly by graduating TS2 students for p5.js and creative coding — they credit it with opening a programming pathway many entered without.

What students named as problematic

xFab (14) is the most-named would-eliminate, but almost no student wants to eliminate xFab itself. The complaints are about pacing and structure — the weekly-rotation "depth vs. breadth" tension (basket-weaving, 3D scanning, the cardboard/Rube Goldberg first weeks) and the online lecture component, which multiple students want dropped or shortened. Several propose swapping specific units (weaving → typography / visual foundations).

CPS (7) is mostly about CPS-2. Students name it as redundant with MS2 or with the thesis sequence, unclear in its goals, or too writing-heavy — one wants it replaced with a mandatory build class. A few flag the specific instructor rather than the course structure.

Critical Computation (5) draws lecture-format and tooling complaints — the CC lecture in Zoom format, and a TS2 student arguing the choice of p5.js over JavaScript / HTML / CSS leaves graduates under-prepared for industry tooling.

Major Studio (4) gets only small, specific critiques: excessive work-sessions / underused class time, the five-and-five midterm pacing model, and one student naming the "Instruction Sets for Strangers" assignment itself.

What gets discussed

AI as a learning aid ~6 students

Students who use AI to understand code or material, not just generate it. MS2-Pirone-S3 frames it as "finding the boundary" for what's still your own work; others stress reading through AI-generated code to learn from it rather than running it blindly.

Program-vs-industry gap ~6 students

The clearest TS2 complaint. "All the interviews I got this summer, they asked me how do you use AI". One graduate calls the program "anti-AI", which "forced critical thinking but left me unprepared for industry AI use". Another finds the silence on AI-in-design-jobs "out of sync with industry interviews".

Deliberate avoidance / restraint ~3 students

Students who choose not to use AI for principled reasons. TS2-MacLow-S1 is ideologically opposed (ecological and pedagogical concerns; wants own small language models, not API forks). Others avoid AI in early coursework to build fundamentals first, then integrate it later.

AI as project element / collaborator ~3 students

Students who want AI to be the subject of work rather than a productivity layer — MS2-Slowik-S5 wants AI used "as a project element / model rather than just content generation"; MS2-Pirone-S5 vibe-coded a systemic-invisibility-of-women health-data project; TS2-Carroll-S5 uses Figma MCP into Cursor as a "junior developer."

Ethical / critical engagement ~3 students

Students who name moral, ecological, or pedagogical concerns about AI. The recurring critique: the program's mission says critical engagement, but the classroom conversation is uneven. MS2-Lavigne-S1: "people seem afraid of talking about it".

The recurring ask

A dedicated AI-as-collaborator course or clearer classroom frameworks — not a ban, not a free-for-all, but explicit guardrails and a culture of critical justification for when and how AI is used. Where the program engages AI seriously (Critical Computation lab, Runway-based electives), students credit it. The complaint isn't the program's stance on AI; it's that the classroom conversation around it is uneven and often missing.

What students credit

Mentoring style & iteration

As an example of this, Harpreet is named 5 times for the way he engages with student work: "there was not a single question that he asked that didn't make sense"; "the way he spoke about design and encouraged me to iterate".

Conceptual / critical rigor

Multiple students credit Major Studio instructors with reshaping how they think — "because of the critical thinking concepts we had to discuss".

Cross-disciplinary expertise

Students name faculty whose own practice opened a path they hadn't seen — speculative design, biodesign and cross-school connections, game-design-to-museum-work bridges. The pattern: an instructor's external work or background gave the student permission and a route into a direction they wouldn't have approached on their own.

Networks & introductions

"The professors are really helpful. They always introduce people who I need". Faculty function as career bridges that the program itself doesn't formally provide.

The structural implication

If half the value lives in who teaches rather than what the courses are, course-design changes won't move the needle on student experience nearly as much as faculty hiring, retention, and section-to-instructor matching. The clearest evidence: "MS1 vs MS2: same course structure, very different experience due to instructor approach. MS2 instructor was a better fit". Students explicitly ask for instructor profiles before section selection, more 1-on-1 faculty time, more professors, and more faculty-led research labs. The fragility risk: when one instructor scales back or leaves, the loss is structural for the students they would have taught.

Career & post-grad readiness

About half the students mention career anxiety, and it ticks up near graduation. "Students want a clearer bridge between studio practice and the post-graduate market."

The shape of the concern

The bridge to industry feels uneven

Students want more help connecting the program to the post-graduate market — internships, alumni network, external collabs, recommended career-path tracks. A few graduating students name the absence directly: "DT doesn't really talk about the money side, or the actual real, lived experience"; "I created an internship for myself in the summer because I wasn't getting any bites". The recurring ask is in Resource requests (the career-pipeline bucket is the single largest at 11 students).

Career anxiety doesn't track satisfaction

Career-anxious students are not the program's unhappy students — many of them are in the "fully achieved my goals" bucket too. Anxiety here is about the world the students are stepping into, not about the program itself.
