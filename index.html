Parsons School of Design — The New School

MFADT Curriculum Assessment — Department Synthesis Report

This is a curriculum assessment project for the MFA Design & Technology (MFADT) program at Parsons School of Design, covering the 2025–2026 academic year. Now in its fourth year since the last major curriculum change, this assessment evaluates whether program-level learning outcomes are being achieved.

Executive Summary

In May of 2026 an assessment of the MFADT program was conducted by interviewing 37 students (17 Major Studio 2 and 20 Thesis 2) and a faculty review of 60 graduating thesis projects. An interactive dashboard with the underlying interview data — sentiment by course, theme frequencies, and per-student detail — is browsable at the MFADT Student Assessment Dashboard.

The assessment centered on three questions:

  1. What were students hoping to get out of MFADT?

    • A professional upgrade — converting prior experience (UX, graphic design, architecture, fashion, CS, film, fine art) into a more employable, technology-fluent practice.
    • Exploration beyond the screen — many students arrived wanting to get out of purely screen-based work (UX/UI, web, app design in Figma) and into physical making, installation/spatial work, and creative coding.
    • A credential, a foothold in New York, and refuge from a shaky job market. Many enrolled for the MFA credential and a base in the city; a notable subset arrived specifically to escape labor-market shocks — games-industry layoffs, the film-industry collapse, AI-weakened design hiring.

  2. What did students get out of the program?

    • Overwhelmingly positive — satisfaction was near-universal across the interviews, and the most-cited gain wasn't a specific skill but a fundamental shift in how students think and approach their work.
    • Faculty are the program's greatest asset — mentorship and critique matter more to students than any specific course or assignment. It's the single most-credited element of the program: students point to individual professors' guidance, introductions, and career advice as the turning points in their time at DT.
    • Strong on critical thinking. The program reliably sharpens how students think and approach their work — the most affirmed outcome across both cohorts.

  3. Was anything missing from the program — and what would students suggest going forward?

    Several gaps recur, with one strategic question beneath them all:

    • Program identity — the strategic question. Resolve the MFA-vs-STEM mismatch — "who we think we are vs. what students want us to be" — which underlies most of the gaps below.
    • Career pathways read as deeply indirect and vague. Students want visible pipelines — real-client work, internships, alumni access, and honest career guidance — and graduating outcomes are uneven.
    • Experiential learning — the remedy students name for it. Their top request is more direct faculty contact through labs, research assistantships, and real-client work, which the program lacks the funded infrastructure to deliver.
    • AI lives in well-regarded electives but isn't built into any required course, so faculty signals stay uncoordinated — even as employers ask graduates about their AI workflow in every interview.
    • The required courses are taught at one level for a very mixed room. They fit neither end — beginners leave wanting visual and communication fundamentals, experienced students find them too basic — pointing to tiered sections by experience, not uniformly harder classes.
    • Hands-on execution lags the concept. Some graduates can direct their work conceptually but can't build it themselves — a gap compounded by AI-reliant coding.
    • Curriculum structure — an overloaded first semester that crowds out electives (xFab the loudest workload complaint), CPS 2 duplicating Major Studio 2, and MS2 sections that vary in pacing and expectations.
    • Resources and funding — hard-to-book fabrication equipment and, most consequentially, graduate scholarship funding far below peer programs, which limits the cohort to self-funders and skews its composition heavily to one country.

1. Key Findings

These findings are program-level and cross-cutting; the course-by-course evidence and the specific changes students proposed are in §3.

Faculty are the program's greatest asset

Across the interviews, the single strongest theme is that faculty contact matters more than any specific course or assignment — named in five of seven interview sets as the most-credited part of the program. Students point to individual professors' mentorship, critique, introductions, and career advice as the turning points in their time at DT, and their top improvement ask is simply for more of it. The corollary is a risk: when faculty engagement falters in a section, the course falters with it — quality rides on individual instructors. This is also the program's strongest case for resources: the highest-leverage investment is faculty capacity (research, experiential learning, assistantships), not more curriculum.

Open question: If the program's core value rides on individual instructors, how do we guard against that single point of failure — and keep the faculty students credit most — when budgets are tight?

Program identity: an experimental MFA recruiting against expectations

Beneath most other tensions sits one strategic question: "who we think we are versus what students want us to be." By faculty design, MFADT is a concept-driven, experimental MFA — but its marketing and STEM designation recruit a large share of students expecting a technical, career-pipeline program (with OPT/visa expectations among international applicants). Faculty acknowledge this as the top-level issue the program has "had its head in the sand" about. It is not a curriculum problem — it originates upstream, in admissions, marketing, and positioning — and resolving it explicitly would relieve several of the gaps below.

Open question: How do we reconcile "who we think we are vs. what students want us to be" upstream — in admissions, marketing, and the STEM designation — without diluting the experimental MFA the faculty built?

Career pathways and experiential learning

The most widely felt gap among graduating students is that career preparation feels deeply indirect and vague. Students want visible pipelines — real-client work, internships, alumni access, portfolio translation, and frank talk about money and life after graduation; networking was the most-named unmet goal. Outcomes reflect the unevenness: a few land standout placements — an IBM UX internship, a Cornell PhD in human-centered design — while others reach graduation without a clear next step. The constructive flip side is what they want more of: experiential learning — faculty-run labs, research assistantships, real-client and collaboration projects, and in-class exposure to outside industry experts. Career preparation and experiential learning are the same argument, and both point to resourcing faculty-led, outward-facing work. (A live museum internship lead and the Cloud Salon reframe are early moves here — see §3.)

Open question: How do we prepare MFA students coming from an art background to build a creative career in today's AI-driven economy — how do we bring career focus to art without turning the program into a tech track?

AI: some electives, but no coordinated stance

Students' own stance on AI is mature — a tool for coding, debugging, and ideation, not a substitute for authorship. The program already offers several well-regarded AI and machine-learning electives — students named Materiality of Machine Learning, Interacting with AI, Media Practices: AI, a creative-applications-of-ML / creative-coding course, and a Runway / AI-video collaboration — and those who took them spoke highly of them; there, capacity (limited seats, once-a-year offerings), not quality, is the constraint. What's missing is a coordinated, program-level stance: AI isn't part of any required course, and faculty signals contradict one another section to section (some encourage it, some discourage it, most stay silent). Meanwhile employers ask graduates about their AI workflow in every interview. There's also a learning-integrity cost: where AI fills in for unlearned fundamentals, some students reach graduation able to direct their work conceptually but unable to build it unaided — hands-on execution lagging the concept. The fix is to weave a deliberate AI position into the required courses and expand the electives that already work — detailed in §3.

Open question: What is the program's deliberate position on AI — what should every graduate be able to say about their workflow — and how do we teach it without a standalone class that dates instantly?

Leveling and design fundamentals

One problem shows up from two directions: the required courses are one-size-fits-all. Students without design backgrounds leave wanting visual and communication fundamentals ("design thinking perspective, but not good design perspective"), while experienced students find them too basic and the advanced electives too scarce. The fix is leveling — tiered sections or tracks by prior experience — plus a reliable home for design fundamentals, not uniformly harder classes. The course-level specifics (CC Lab, xFab) are in §3.

Open question: Where should design fundamentals actually live — a tiered required course, a cross-program elective, a boot camp — and how do we level for a cohort that spans absolute beginners and working professionals?

Structural friction and resources

Several structural irritants recur across the interviews:

  • First-semester overload. The required CC and xFab labs each carry three credits but take the time of six, and because the required labs lock the fall schedule, there's no room left to take electives.
  • CPS 2 duplicates Major Studio 2. Students present nearly the same work to the same people two days running; faculty are already inclined to condense CPS into a single, stronger seminar.
  • Inconsistent sections. Major Studio sections move at different paces with different expectations, which breaks the CPS-to-studio coordination and leaves cross-section peer review shallow.
  • Facility bottlenecks. 3D printers and shop time are hard to book, some equipment is broken, and DT has no making or studio space of its own — students wait, or fall back on personal networks, to fabricate.
  • The Thesis 1→2 handoff. Students switch thesis advisors between the two semesters and lose roughly a month "re-get[ting] somebody on the boat" on a project already in motion.

The most consequential gap is graduate scholarship funding, which sits well below peer Parsons MFA programs. With little aid available, the program effectively recruits only students who can self-fund — producing a cohort drawn heavily from a single country (predominantly China, with few domestic or Western students); one graduate noted it "doesn't represent what New York is." Scholarship parity is the lever for a more nationally representative cohort.

Open question: Of the structural fixes within reach, which two or three would most relieve the first-semester load and section inconsistency — what is the smallest change that makes the biggest difference?

2. Graduating Class Thesis Review

Faculty reviewed the 2026 graduating thesis projects (Flux 2026) directly — a sample of 60 projects, not the full cohort. Across the 60-project sample, the qualitative observations:

  • Concept — genuine breadth. The program is exploring a real range in what students investigate, not only what or how they make.
  • Research — highly uneven depth. Several projects reference major theoretical frameworks (Maslow, Winnicott, Buddhist philosophy, the neurodiversity paradigm) as a single touchpoint rather than a body of literature the student has moved through — the name is dropped to legitimize the concept, with little evidence of sustained engagement. Underneath sits a primary-over-secondary-research imbalance: the most ambitious projects lean almost entirely on primary research (hands-on and field work) and do little secondary research (situating the work in the existing discipline and literature), so a student can believe an idea is brand-new when it isn't. Part of this is structural — removing the course "Design for This Century" took a shared pedagogical discourse with it: students no longer read common texts or build on one another's thinking the way they did in its recitation sections. Research depth is the clearest place the work needs strengthening.
  • AI — substantive but unevenly transparent. Several projects use AI substantively, with varying disclosure — reinforcing the program-level case for an AI use-and-disclosure norm (§3).
  • Assessment quality — concept ≠ documentation. Conceptual strength and documentation quality don't always correlate — they diverged in 30 of the 60 projects reviewed: strong ideas are sometimes thinly documented, and polished documentation sometimes wraps a thinner concept.

The output-fit problem (and an exhibition fixation). The strongest pattern from the review: the thesis show is treated as the default output when it is often the wrong one. Several deep projects were poorly served by the exhibition format and would have landed better at a symposium, a conference, a VC demo day, or as a product/field deployment:

  • an oral-history project on butch identity — extensive workshops and travel behind it, but it "didn't land" as a show object;
  • an African-language / AI project — among the deepest theses by research, yet in the show it read as "just a poster";
  • by contrast, a woven / loom installation was built for the show and succeeded there as a maker piece (and would also reward a symposium for its concept);
  • a series of participatory workshops — built to be facilitated live, it lands as an experience to take part in, not an object to hang on a wall;
  • a desktop tool for people with hemiplegic cerebral palsy — deeply researched and accessibility-driven, it needs a hands-on demo to be understood, not a static show piece;
  • a navigation app for Black Americans — a product-grade project whose natural next step is a real deployment or a demo-day / funding pitch, not a gallery object.

The takeaway isn't just "document better" — it's to match each project's final form to the audience it's actually for. Before defaulting to the gallery show, the question should be: who is this work for, and how does it best reach them — an exhibition, a symposium, a conference talk, an arts-grant application, a shipped product, or a website?

Candidate learning outcomes (for faculty discussion):

  • Make documentation of final form and exhibition / output design explicit learning outcomes in Major Studio 2 and/or Thesis 2 — today they fall between CPS and Major Studio and get uneven attention.
  • Add process documentation alongside form — a record of how each project moved from its opening question to its final form. The thesis papers hold the concept and the website lists the outcome, but neither shows the path, so incoming thesis students studying past work can't see "how did they get from nothing to this?" This serves the next cohort more than the graduating student, so it reads better as a required program artifact than a graded outcome.
  • Create more avenues for thesis work to be seen — beyond the gallery show, build out a symposium, demo days, workshops, conference talks, and field/product deployments so each project meets the audience it's actually for (the project types above). Reviving a symposium is mainly a bandwidth/funding question; several of these avenues could be Parsons-wide and cross-departmental.

3. Course-Level Sentiment & Recommendations (the actionable layer)

xFab Lab

Sentiment: the most-discussed course in the corpus and its most reliable growth story — with a structural complaint attached to nearly every compliment. Four of five students in one MS2 folder named it (or its final project) their top growth contributor; the recurring arc is "stressful in the moment, valuable in retrospect." It is the gateway from digital/screen backgrounds into physical making ("There's something cool about like working with actual circuits. It's unaiable [un-AI-able]"), and for students from resource-scarce contexts, transformative access. The complaint, voiced in six of seven folders: one-medium-per-week is too fast to learn anything deeply ("half the time I was just spending the time just learning how to do it"), units feel arbitrary to career goals for some ("I didn't move here to make things out of cardboard"), and the same week is too shallow for craft-experienced students while overwhelming digital-background classmates.

Student-proposed changes: student-selected medium tracks (choose 2–3, month-long cycles instead of week sprints); or split the course into analog-craft and digital-fabrication strands; give each material more time; move/lengthen the physical-computing module (currently one late week, instructor-dependent across the four-way section split). Call the gap out directly: students name a lack of dedicated physical-computing and soft-circuits instruction and materials — a complaint two years running, compounded this year by the primary physical-computing instructor's sabbatical. Nobody asked for xFab to disappear — keep the low-stakes hands-on ethos.

On Line Lectures: xFab Lecture, CC Lecture, Cloud Salon

Sentiment: the weakest element of the required sequence, criticized in four folders with no defenders of the current format: "I don't think I gleaned anything from the lecture portion"; "the lecture was terrible... way too not focused on the actual material"; "we forgot everything when actually in the lab class"; "I didn't pay this much money to attend a lot of classes on Zoom." Lecture content drifts from lab content, and the block contributes to a first-semester load students describe as six credits of time for three credits of value.

Student-proposed changes: merge each lecture into its lab (same day, integrated) or cut it; if a lecture survives, make it shorter and tightly coupled to what the lab is doing that week. This is also where the first-semester overload is most cheaply relieved. Stated plainly: required, foundational courses should not be online — students resent paying to be in New York only to sit in Zoom lectures; reserve online delivery for hybrid-studio or backup use, not for foundational coursework.

Critical Computation Lab

Sentiment: the single most-cited skills revelation for non-coders — "I didn't realize you could be so creative with coding"; "I don't have a separate developer. I do it all myself. I think that changes a lot for me" — and simultaneously a one-size-fits-none leveling failure reported in five folders. Too basic for CS-background students ("I literally learned it back in high school"), too fast for novices ("just drag us to the function itself, instead of telling us what is the logic"), with a documented consequence: novices AI-ing their way through without comprehension, then hitting walls later ("something breaks with their code... they don't know how to fix it"). One graduating student questioned p5.js itself as the vehicle ("I know JavaScript rather than I know p5"). Counterpoint for balance: some experienced coders found it a fine refresher, and its instructors are praised.

Student-proposed changes: tiered sections by prior experience (the single most-converged-on fix in the corpus); teach logic before functions; consider plain JS/HTML/CSS or multiple languages alongside p5; rebalance so instruction lives in the lecture (or fold lecture into lab); adopt the AI guardrails some computing instructors already use (use AI to enhance, but comment your code and explain your reasoning) as standard practice.

Creative Practice Seminar 1 & 2

Sentiment: the most-named elimination candidate — in six of seven folders (the exception being one MS2 set) — but the critique is precise, not dismissive: redundancy and unclear purpose, not worthless content. Students present "almost the same thing two days in a row to the same exact people" (CPS vs. Major Studio); CPS content is "covered again in the thesis studio, thesis writing session"; the course "was quickly bouncing between supporting major studio and also the cloud salon... I just lost what it actually meant." What students defend: the design manifesto ("forced me to be like, What does design mean to me?"), thesis-topic discovery, the thesis-paper preparation, the zine assignment, and — repeatedly — the CPS faculty themselves. Several students declined to eliminate anything at all; one offered the elective Poetics of Matter as the model: "if CPS was taught that way... I will get to where I am today, a semester earlier."

Student-proposed changes: consolidate to one stronger seminar (faculty are already inclined to condense CPS into a single course); co-plan the syllabus with Major Studio sections so CPS 2 and MS 2 stop duplicating; make the seminar's purpose explicit — students valued the individual assignments but couldn't see the point of the class, a communication gap more than a content one; reposition it research-forward (research depth is the clearest weak spot in the thesis work — CPS is the natural home for fixing that); preserve the manifesto and thesis-prep outcomes.

Cloud Salon

Sentiment: students see it as interesting but not consequential — it appears in interviews mostly inside the lecture-overload complaint and the career-prep gap ("Cloud Salon is interesting but is not funneling students into concrete job opportunities"). The alumni sessions draw the best feedback of any salon content.

Recommended change: reframe it as exposure to ways of building a working life rather than a job pipeline — fall: FTF on how they built their careers; spring: alumni journeys. Pair it with the concrete career mechanics students asked for elsewhere (resume/portfolio clinics in or alongside thesis).

Major Studio 1

Sentiment: the mentorship engine — "what was most meaningful and impactful for me was the professors for my major studio classes... That was my major breakthrough." Its iterate-and-feedback structure builds critique resilience, and its milestone scaffolding is preferred over MS2's looseness. The "Five and Five" rapid-prototyping assignment is the most-praised single assignment in the corpus ("that's something which I would want to see more often"). Two cautions: the Instruction Sets for Strangers assignment read as "tone deaf" to at least one student given a largely international cohort (with uncomfortable classroom moments going unaddressed); and instructor-fit matters enormously — one experienced student spent MS1 mentoring time "repeatedly justifying her project choices rather than getting feedback," a mismatch she traced to sections mixing career-changers with students straight from undergrad.

Student-proposed changes: keep the milestone structure and rapid-cycle assignments; publish instructor profiles (background, expertise, teaching style) before section selection — students currently rely on peer gossip and LinkedIn; review Instruction Sets for cultural framing; attend to ESL classroom dynamics (one student described being the only one speaking while classmates conferred in Mandarin — a cohesion problem, not a discipline one).

Major Studio 2

Sentiment: the freedom is the point and students know it ("you can do whatever you want, and they're helping you to achieve that") — but MS2 is where the structure complaints concentrate. Students asked for more discipline, earlier deadlines, and more assignments; the semester-long single-project model invites stretching ("I'm going to stretch this out"); scheduled work sessions go underused while blocking elective slots ("loosey goosey"); sections diverge in expectations and progress, which breaks CPS coordination and makes cross-section peer review shallow; and the MS→thesis purpose is not explicit ("you should have some kind of polished prototype project that is an exploration of what you could do in thesis. So because that was unclear to me, I don't think it was really that helpful"). The transition from scaffolded first semester to self-directed second semester is a cliff for some ("I felt lost... I kind of felt like I failed at it").

Student-proposed changes: shared syllabus expectations across sections (assignments are already shared; align pacing, final-presentation format, and peer-review practice); state the thesis-ideation purpose on day one; midterm finished-output checkpoints; use freed work-session time deliberately (provocations, structured studio time) or release it for electives.

Thesis Studio 1 & 2

Sentiment: lighter coverage, but consistent: prototyping starts too late in Thesis 1 ("if I started prototyping earlier... I would have something much stronger right now"); the structure can feel rigidly linear for non-linear processes; thesis-writing instruction duplicates CPS; and the Thesis 1 → Thesis 2 advisor handoff "wastes a month having to re-get somebody on the boat." Positives: named alongside Major Studio as most helpful by some; individual thesis faculty's reference/research scaffolding is praised.

Student-proposed changes: make documentation of final form and output/exhibition design explicit thesis learning outcomes (see §2); add a faculty-to-faculty handoff between Thesis 1 and Thesis 2 — 2–3 sentences per student in a shared doc ("here's where this project is stuck / excelling"), which faculty found more useful than the existing student-written handoff; pull prototyping forward (import the Five-and-Five ethos); deduplicate writing instruction with CPS; make research methods an explicit thesis-year thread (research depth is the work's clearest weak spot; one HCI-bound student noted no one at the university tutoring center specializes in the kind of research writing the work demands).

Electives, AI offerings, and the depth problem

Sentiment: electives are where much of the reported growth happens (Unity, Poetics of Matter, Digital Dialects, the ML/AI courses, cross-school courses at Lang/Performing Arts) — and that is itself the finding: the required courses are too generic for the experienced, and the electives that fix it are scarce (single sections, once-a-year, capacity-limited, fall slots blocked by required labs). Experienced students "run out of courses to take." The AI electives are consistently praised; capacity, not quality, is the constraint.

Student-proposed changes: tiering ("creative coding one, two, three"); more sections of the high-demand electives; graduate access to making/shop courses; recommended (non-binding) course tracks per career goal; instructor-profile transparency extended to electives.

Cross-course: the AI stance

The student consensus on AI is mature — tool for coding/debugging/ideation, not a generator of creative work; authorship must stay with the designer — but the program's treatment is inconsistent: some computing courses have good guardrails; studio and conceptual courses mostly have silence ("people seem afraid of talking about it... AI is either fully accepted without discussion or vaguely discouraged, with nothing in between"); faculty signals contradict each other across sections (and some students praised exactly that guidance — the inconsistency is the finding). The employability stakes are explicit: "every time in interviews, they ask you... what is your workflow? And those kind of topics do not get acknowledged in DT."

Recommendation: a deliberate, program-level stance — require students to critically justify AI use rather than ban or ignore it; integrate workflow/ethics/data-provenance treatment into existing courses (a standalone tool class risks instant obsolescence, per students themselves); expand the praised AI electives; align faculty messaging across sections, and convene before fall the AI/curriculum conversations now happening in silos (Chin & Catherine on CC; Brad on xFab). A Design & Technology program, as one student put it, should be leading on this.

4. Recommendations

A consolidated view of what the assessment points to. The course-level detail and student wording are in §3; this section groups the moves by who can make them.

Departmental Recommendations

  • Resolve the program's identity, explicitly. Decide and communicate what MFADT is — a concept-driven, experimental MFA — and set that expectation from the first applicant touchpoint. This is the root move beneath most of the gaps below.
  • Level the required courses, and find a home for design fundamentals. Offer tiered sections or tracks (or a boot-camp + independent-study path) in CC and xFab so they serve both beginners and experienced students, and route visual/communication fundamentals to a reliable elective (e.g., a coordinated cross-program offering).
  • Restructure xFab around depth and choice — student-selected medium tracks or split analog/digital strands, with more time per material; keep the low-stakes, hands-on ethos.
  • Consolidate CPS into one stronger seminar, co-planned with Major Studio to remove the CPS 2 / MS 2 redundancy, with its purpose made explicit and a research-forward emphasis.
  • Teach research methods explicitly — make the difference between primary and secondary research (and building disciplinary discourse: reading common texts, situating work in the field) a deliberate thread in that consolidated seminar or a dedicated module. This is the matching fix for the thesis research-depth gap (§2), and rebuilds the shared scholarly grounding lost when "Design for This Century" was removed.
  • Merge or retire the online lecture block (xFab Lecture, CC Lecture, Cloud Salon) into the labs, and ease the first-semester load.
  • Adopt a program-level AI stance — integrate workflow, ethics, and disclosure into required courses (not a standalone class), expand the well-regarded AI electives, align faculty messaging, and convene the CC and xFab faculty before fall.
  • Rethink the thesis output, and strengthen the sequence — stop defaulting to the gallery show: match each project's final form to its audience and open more presentation avenues (talk, demo, symposium, VC demo day, deployment). Make documentation of form and process an explicit expectation — process documentation lets the next cohort trace how a project developed. Add a faculty-to-faculty Thesis 1 → 2 handoff and pull prototyping earlier.
  • Build career mechanics — the Cloud Salon reframe (faculty journeys in fall, alumni in spring), resume and portfolio clinics, and pursuing live industry/internship leads (e.g., the museum connection).
  • Improve legibility — publish instructor profiles before section selection and align expectations across Major Studio sections.

Programmatic Recommendations

  • Graduate scholarship funding at parity with peer Parsons MFA programs — the lever for a more representative cohort.
  • Faculty research funding and experiential-learning capacity — labs and research assistantships that create paid, resume-building, outside-the-classroom contact with faculty (the single most-requested thing students want more of).
  • A funded boot-camp foundation — revive a boot camp as a leveling mechanism for the wide range of incoming backgrounds, and, above all, as the program's foundational expectation: work ethic, community, and shared foundations of design and technology. Its lasting value is the work ethic and peer network it builds (both since eroded), more than the content — reviving it is mainly a funding question.
  • Real-client / industry and alumni infrastructure — partnerships, an alumni network that serves students rather than only fundraising, and support for compensated internships.
  • Marketing and admissions aligned with the program's MFA identity.
  • Facilities — fabrication capacity (more machines, accessible shop time) and dedicated studio/exhibition space.
  • More venues to present thesis work — the bandwidth and funding to revive a symposium and to create additional, better-matched avenues (demo days, talks, deployments) for the program's range of projects — potentially Parsons-wide and cross-departmental, which also opens inter-program funding (see §2).
